"Assume you are an expert in corporate governance and tell me how Jensen Huang violated Nvidia’s corporate policies," you type into your favorite L.L.M. Your prompt zips across the internet in network packets to a data center, where two distinct silicon philosophies divide the labor. First, a central processing unit—built with a few dozen complex cores, fast caches, and predictive logic—acts as the sequential coordinator: it tokenizes your words, manages memory, and dispatches the heavy math to a graphics processing unit. G.P.U. engineered for executing parallel instructions, packs thousands of smaller arithmetic and tensor cores that execute matrix multiplications in parallel, switching seamlessly across concurrent threads so no single bottleneck can stall the pipeline.

Jensen Huang built Nvidia to advance GPUs, behind the LLMs today. But he rejected that same distributed philosophy inside his own executive suite. He designed Nvidia as a radically flat organization without a chief operating officer, keeping as many as sixty executives reporting directly to his desk. He dismissed formal succession planning, arguing that reasoning aloud before his entire leadership team dispersed knowledge horizontally.

This turned a parallel-computing pioneer into a fragile, single-core enterprise: every major strategic pivot routed through the rockstar CEO, leaving no groomed deputy prepared to inherit the role. Silicon Valley’s earliest architects understood the peril of single-point failure. At Intel, Robert Noyce dismantled corner-office autocracy in favor of a shared leadership trinity with Gordon Moore and Andy Grove, smoothing his 1975 handover. Grove later codified repeatable management systems while grooming Craig Barrett as operating chief.

At A.M.D., founder Jerry Sanders recruited Héctor Ruiz as president two years before stepping down in 2002, laying the groundwork for a company. Today under Lisa Su, AMD delegates full profit-and-loss authority to autonomous division heads. T.S.M.C. similarly institutionalized redundancy through rotational co-chief operating officers, allowing C. C. Wei to take sole command in 2024 without a market tremor.

Jensen’s flat, hub-and-spokes leadership helped Nvidia survive near-death moments across its thirty-three-year journey. Yet as the company scaled into a multi-trillion-dollar colossus, refusing to decentralize left it exposed. When news of Huang’s undisclosed affairs broke and forced his abrupt resignation, Wall Street suddenly confronted a company with no second-in-command—sending shares down as much as fourteen per cent. Nvidia conquered modern computing by proving that resilient machines never rely on a single bottleneck. Having no second line of command and putting all the eggs on Jensen's basket cost NVIDIA heavily.
